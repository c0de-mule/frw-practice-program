An Interactive Companion to This Field Guide

WORDS TO TAP BY

Why Tap? Protocols that combine tapping on acupressure points with psychological interventions, such as the Emotional Freedom Techniques (EFT) and Thought Field Therapy (TFT), share with all therapies the overriding goals of relieving emotional suffering and optimizing the client’s capacity to flourish. What has drawn practitioners to tapping protocols is how quickly change often comes, how well the gains hold, and the broad range of problems they reach.

What Tapping Does in the Brain. Tapping protocols pair a technique that sends regulating signals to the nervous system with language that keeps the problem active, both in the client’s mind and in the brain regions that encode it. The electrochemical signals generated by the tapping then reach those regions. The body stays regulated while the distressing material is still engaged. The nervous system expects distress but instead meets calm, and that experienced mismatch, the trigger for what researchers call memory reconsolidation, is what makes the change durable. The earlier learning that drove a maladaptive reaction is revised in that moment rather than merely quieted.

The Fundamental Role of Language. Language determines which brain regions are active when the signals arrive, and therefore what the signals act on. But the words do more than aim the tapping. The right phrase can leave a client feeling understood, bring up what lies beneath the presenting problem, or open a way forward that wasn’t visible a moment earlier. These are three different tasks, and each calls for its own kind of wording. Word choice can be the difference between a session that soothes and one that transforms.

When It’s Your Turn to Speak. Tapping practitioners usually complete their training with the acupoint sequences firmly established. The harder part arrives each time it’s your turn to guide. The client is poised to tap on the next point. What do you say? That moment comes dozens of times in a session, with only a breath or two to decide, and each answer shapes where the session goes next.

A Gap in the Research. For all its importance, therapist language during tapping has remained largely unstudied. It is usually taught through the Setup Statement and Reminder Phrases, along with general principles, instructor examples, and supervised practice — but with no systematic framework for the language variations themselves. That is left to be developed intuitively with clinical experience.

A Decade of Investigation. This Field Guide is the product of a decade-long investigation into the wording used during tapping sessions. It began with a survey of experienced practitioners and went on to code therapist language in recorded sessions, statement by statement. An AI analysis of guided tapping videos was consistent with what the coding had shown. What the investigation found is that skilled practitioners, each speaking in their own voice, draw on the same small set of patterns: three directions a phrase can take (attune, explore, or lead), and twenty-one variations of wording that serve them. Those directions and variations can be taught.

The Compass and the Map. The three directions and twenty-one variations are not a script to follow. Think of them instead as a compass paired with a map. The compass points in one of the three directions, revealing where the next phrase should head. The map shows the seven language paths that take you in the direction of attuning, the seven for exploring, and the seven for leading. This Guide is designed to make both second nature, so that with a client in front of you, you sense which way to head and have the wording patterns that will get you there already in you.

The Right Words

What are the right words?

The very term “the right words” may sound presumptuous — as if we’re saying only one correct phrase exists. We’re not. In a given moment, any of many phrasing possibilities can serve while others may stall or even interfere with progress. What separates the ones that serve is not eloquence. Eleven qualities mark the difference:

  • The Right Words set a direction — Attune, Explore, or Lead. The coding found just three directions an effective phrase can take, and the choice is made phrase by phrase, not once for the whole session. Attuning meets clients where they are, exploring opens the story beneath the presenting problem, and leading points explicitly toward change.
  • The Right Words carry that direction forward. Once the direction is set, the wording is what advances it — deepening attunement, opening exploration, or leading toward change.
  • The Right Words are often the client’s own. The client’s wording is the default raw material, and while arousal is high it is often all that is needed. Your own wording increasingly enters as intensity decreases and exploration deepens.
  • The Right Words are concrete rather than abstract. Where the material allows, specific, sensory language — tight, heavy, burning, a knot, a weight on my chest — keeps the client in contact with the actual feeling, memory, or sensation, rather than narrating it from a distance.
  • The Right Words are brief. Short enough to be spoken, repeated, or held in mind while tapping a single point. They leave room for the felt experience itself rather than competing with it. Where an idea is too complex for a short phrase, spread its parts across successive tapping points.
  • The Right Words keep the client safe. They keep clients within their window of tolerance — the range in which arousal stays workable. Paired with tapping, they help the nervous system register enough safety to stay engaged with difficult material.
  • The Right Words know when to wait. There is a place for language and a place for silence. When arousal is very high — an 8, 9, or 10 on the SUD (Subjective Units of Distress) scale — the tapping and the breath can often carry the healing on their own. Tapping in silence beside the client is a nonverbal way of attuning. If you do speak, say only what is plainly true of this moment. Generally, this means naming the feeling and stopping there. Some approaches, such as Thought Field Therapy, minimize the use of language, and many EFT practitioners begin working with a charged memory by tapping with no words, or simply on a word that names the feeling, until the intensity drops. As the charge eases and the client can reflect on what is happening, nuanced wording becomes a more valuable tool, and that is where this Guide focuses.
  • The Right Words are offered, not imposed. You supply the phrase, but it only works if the client finds it fits. Returning often to attunement, and particularly as the session enters new or shaky ground, can verify that the words are tethered to the client’s actual experience rather than only your read of it.
  • The Right Words adjust to the moment. The words are chosen fresh rather than recited from a script. They follow the client’s shifting state and focus. What makes fresh choices possible in the moment is a framework absorbed in advance: knowing the directions well enough to sense which one is called for, and having taken in enough effective wording that good phrasing comes more readily. How the Words Arrive, below, explains why.
  • The Right Words begin with presence. Their deepest source is pre-verbal: an attuned, embodied sense of the client and what the moment calls for that often determines the next direction before it has been consciously selected.
  • The Right Words need only be “good enough,” not polished or perfect. Wording that has the above qualities, even roughly, and is offered with presence and a willingness to adjust, can carry the work forward. Whatever one phrase misses, the next can pick up.

The Setup Statement and the Reminder Phrase. Most EFT training begins with two language elements built on a set form. The form stays the same, but the words that fill it do not, and the qualities above apply to those words as much as to any others.

The Setup Statement. It follows a simple form, “Even though [the problem], . . .” What completes it can be a statement of self-acceptance (“Even though I hate it when my father criticizes me, I deeply and completely accept myself”), of trust in the client’s own goodness and inner wisdom (“. . . , I trust the part of me that knows I’m more than his judgments”), or of choice (“. . . , I choose to stay steady in myself despite his judgments”). Naming the problem is itself a form of attuning. A choice adds something more. Rather than simply countering the problem, a well-made choice names a response the client genuinely wants, and “I choose” casts them as an active participant in the change. Because it doesn’t claim the change has already happened, it is also easier to believe than “I am.” And because it is spoken while the problem is still active, the aim is for the preferred response to become more available the next time the old trigger arises.

The Reminder Phrase. In the basic protocol, a short phrase naming the problem (“this anger,” “this fear of flying”) is repeated at each tapping point to keep the client in contact with it. Repeated unchanged, it holds the focus steady, but it also tends to keep the session circling the same ground rather than following the material as it shifts or venturing into what lies beneath it. Skilled practitioners vary the phrase from point to point as the session unfolds, often intuitively. Once it changes with the moment, it is no longer simply a reminder. It becomes a way of moving the session, by attuning, exploring, or leading. The phrases in this Guide map that variation.

How the Words Arrive

What happens in the moment before you speak

Deciding what to say, and how to say it, can be built on an understanding of how the mind produces speech.

Every phrase you offer is the work of two cognitive mechanisms, and they operate in order. Both rest on presence and close listening to the client’s words and to what the client conveys nonverbally.

The first is what language researchers call the “conceptualizer.” It settles what the next statement needs to do. What takes shape at this stage is an intention, not yet a sentence. No words have been chosen. For instance, should the next statement attune to the client and the issue, explore what hasn’t yet been examined, or lead toward what is becoming possible? That decision requires attention, and in a live session, where you direct your attention is among the most consequential choices you make for what the session can achieve. What you notice shapes what you choose. Attend to the client’s distress and you will tend to attune; attend to what their story leaves out and you will tend to explore; catch the first signs of relief and readiness, and leading becomes possible.

The second mechanism is what language researchers call the “formulator.” Once the intention is set, the words come, and they come fast, two or three a second. These words are drawn from a working vocabulary of about thirty thousand for an educated adult, as well as from an uncounted number of patterns laid down by wordings you have encountered in the past. They are assembled into a phrase for your client to repeat, yet that assembling is not something you consciously do.

You will notice only the words as you say them, and if you reflect on it, they will seem to have come from nowhere. They did not. They came without deliberation, but in service of the direction you had just set, whether you reasoned it out or felt it. That is the hinge this Field Guide turns on. Searching harder for just the right words is what the moment may seem to ask of you, but that is a dead end because the words are not where the choosing occurs. No one selects words one by one — not you, not anyone. What you can select is the direction the words will move the session in that moment. And between sessions, through what you read, hear, and practice, you can widen the range of patterns available to your formulator.

The Two Goals

What this Guide helps you do

Each of these two mechanisms suggests a goal for your practice. Helping you reach both is the purpose of this Field Guide.

  1. Meet each moment with a few clear directions rather than an open field. Without a framework, the conceptualizer faces an open-ended search. The three directions narrow it to a question you can answer in a breath: attune, explore, or lead? The seven variations under each are the paths that lead in that direction. Once you know them well, you will sense in the moment which direction is called for and which paths can take you there.
  1. Have the wording already in you. The examples in this Guide are what bring that wording to your formulator. Your training, and your experience of what has worked for clients, have already stocked your formulator. Reading these examples attentively adds new effective patterns to it, much as people who read good writing tend to write better. The patterns will later surface on their own — in your voice and fitted to the client in front of you. That is not memorization but a different kind of learning, the same implicit kind by which you came to speak your native language without being able to state its rules. Once you have taken the examples in deeply, they leave their imprint on the formulator, and you can let them go.

What you absorb from these pages is what will be there when there is no time to deliberate.

The Compass

Three directions for the next phrase

Moment by moment, sense whether the next phrase should attune, explore, or lead. In general, within the work on any given issue, the trajectory begins with attuning. It opens into exploring as resonance is established and safety deepens. It moves toward leading as the SUD lowers and readiness builds. This mirrors Milton Erickson’s principle of pacing and leading, with attuning and exploring as forms of pacing. When pacing has been faithful, leading often follows naturally. These directions are not, however, a fixed sequence. A session can and does cycle back and forth among them as the moment requires. You never have to go the full distance with one before moving to another. At any point, the only question is which direction the client needs now.

Attune

Meet and reflect

Connect with the client and reflect their present experience — building rapport and establishing safety, engagement, and resonance with the client, the presenting problem, and related concerns. Done well, this alone often lowers distress.

Explore

Question and uncover

Bring into awareness the underlying story, patterns, causes, and assumptions — tracing the present difficulty to its roots, uncovering the logic behind it, and examining other factors that play into current challenges. Done well, it brings to light what has been driving the problem, often for the first time.

Lead

Orient toward change

Once distress has eased and readiness has grown, point toward pathways to greater resilience, resolution, and meaningful change. Leading often extends beyond recovery to new growth. Done well, it leaves the client drawn toward the next step rather than pushed toward it.

The Map

Twenty-one paths, seven per direction

Each direction is served by seven variations of wording, and each variation is illustrated below with three examples. For the most part, a variation belongs to a single direction (Attune, Explore, or Lead), though the direction a particular statement serves can shift with the clinical moment. In fact, two — humor and metaphor — while listed under the directions where they most frequently appear, are also at home attuning, exploring, or leading.

Seven is not a magic number. It is the fewest categories we could find that covered every statement in the recorded sessions that served a clear clinical purpose.

Staying With and Going Beyond. The following example phrases originate with the practitioner, but they are written in the first person because it is the client who repeats them aloud. Many are adapted from the client’s own words. When a phrase accurately goes beyond what the client has explicitly revealed, the client often feels deeply heard, showing relief or appreciation along with a readiness to engage further. However, such phrases are always hypotheses. Resonance — a nod, a sigh, a softening, or the client repeating the phrase with feeling — is usually confirmation enough, but when it is missing, settle the question in the moment with a simple “True or false?” or “Is that right?”

Phrases, Not Questions. This Guide focuses on the phrases you offer for the client to repeat while tapping. A few are themselves a form of inquiry (“I wonder what my body is trying to tell me with this back pain”), and they appear throughout. The questions you ask between rounds — opening the session, setting goals, checking the SUD, closing — matter just as much, but they are outside the scope of this Guide.

A Note on Scope. These examples come from clinical practice and the published literature, including work with trauma. Because tapping can bring intense material to the surface quickly, even in a session that wasn’t about trauma, some of the examples may help you respond well when that happens. They are not, however, a substitute for specialized training. When what surfaces is more than you’re equipped to handle, keep the client safe and grounded and refer when needed.

One Phrase, Several Points. While some examples are written here as a sentence or a long phrase, in practice such longer wording can be spread across two, three, or more tapping points.

Seven Variations of Attuning Language

Attune

Words that attune acknowledge the client’s inner experience and hopes and reflect these back accurately, communicating that the client’s world is understood and respected. This includes what feels contradictory or hard to hold. It is where the therapeutic relationship begins and where the work returns regularly throughout a session. While the following seven variations of words that attune are numbered, they might occur in any order.

1. Naming the Problem

Phrases that describe a client’s feelings, challenges, or haunting memories signal that their inner reality has been heard. When something a client has been carrying alone, or has been unable to put into words, is accurately named, the naming itself can bring relief. The isolation around it eases, and the feeling becomes something that can be tapped on rather than held at bay. On the other hand, prematurely reaching for a positive framing of the client’s challenges can ring false and invite resistance. Examples:

  • This grief that just won’t lift.
  • The shame I feel when I think about the accident.
  • My throat tightens when I imagine facing him.

2. Validating Reasons to Keep the Problem

Coping strategies that once helped may have become ineffective and often counterproductive. Acknowledging why a pattern is hard to relinquish despite its costs builds empathy, reduces resistance, and paves the way for a shift. An open-ended phrase the client completes can begin that acknowledgment, e.g., “It’s no wonder that I . . .” Examples:

  • My perfectionism has helped me succeed.
  • This worry keeps me alert — nothing bad can sneak up on me.
  • Maintaining distance protects me from being hurt again.

3. Recognizing Conflicting Feelings

Conflicting feelings sit side by side. Neither cancels the other. The task is simply to make room for both. Each side gets its full say. Early in treatment, the conflict may even be about tapping itself. Examples:

  • I want help, and I don’t see how tapping on my skin is going to do anything.
  • I love my wife, and I deeply resent her.
  • I want to forgive, but I’m still so angry I can barely speak to him.

4. Discerning Multiple Layers

Unlike conflicting feelings, which sit side by side, here one feeling lies beneath another. Hidden though these deeper layers may be, they can be named once the client is beginning to sense them. A phrase that names one gives words to what is just starting to surface. Examples:

  • When I tune into this anger, the sadness underneath it is overwhelming.
  • Part of me feels relieved it’s over — but beneath that relief is grief I’m just touching into.
  • Behind all this busyness is a loneliness I haven’t wanted to face.

5. Using the Client’s Exact Words

The client’s own phrasing is often the most direct way to stay connected to the issue. Words are anchors: the client’s own words connect directly to their meanings and felt experience. You can give it back word for word — a single charged word or a whole sentence exactly as they said it — rather than paraphrasing into your own language. Examples:

  • If the client says, “I feel crushed,” use “crushed.”
  • If the client says, “I’m drowning in pressure,” repeat “drowning in pressure.”
  • If the client says, “There’s no one coming to help me,” tap the sentence whole.

6. Softening Harsh Absolutes

Extreme statements can be modified to make the issue more workable, while staying true to the client’s experience. Start by tapping on their exact wording. As the charge comes down, the statement can be reframed in a less absolute form. Examples:

  • From“No one can be trusted” To “Right now, it seems impossible to trust.”
  • From“This situation is hopeless” To “At this moment, I don’t see a way forward.”
  • From“Nothing works” To “I haven’t found a good solution.”

7. Using Humor or Irony

Used carefully, humor can produce a leap in engagement — but only when the alliance is solid and the client has shown some lightness about the issue. Poorly timed, humor can shatter trust. It appears most often as attuning because its first job is connection. A joke that lands shows you understand the client’s experience well enough to exaggerate it accurately, and shared laughter puts you side by side, looking at the problem together from a little distance. Humor can also open exploration or support leading. Examples:

  • If self-criticism were an Olympic sport, I’d have retired undefeated.
  • My anxiety shows up early to everything, just in case the disaster starts without us.
  • I’m everyone’s emergency contact, but somehow I didn’t get the number to call.

Seven Variations of Exploring Language

Explore

Once attunement has established safety and connection, language can begin to open doors — bringing hidden aspects into awareness, examining recurring patterns, questioning old assumptions, and linking present difficulty to past experience. Exploration often requires a hypothesis that reaches past what the client has put into words. If you are making an informed guess and the client’s response doesn’t show that it landed well, follow it with a confirmation question (such as “True or false?”) to keep attunement intact.

1. Connecting Past and Present

Linking current feelings and events to earlier experiences illuminates the roots of an issue. Many therapists use a technique called the affect bridge. The client tunes into the feeling a current situation evokes and follows it back, as if across a bridge, to an earlier time they felt the same way, often one of the first. The tapping can then turn to that earlier event. Simpler prompts can also connect present and past. You can ask, “What does this remind you of?” Or, a few rounds in, offer an open-ended phrase to tap on: “This reminds me of . . .” Examples:

  • When my boss criticizes me, I go right back to never being good enough for Dad.
  • The panic I feel every time is like the moment I realized the car was about to crash.
  • This shame reminds me of how I felt when my teacher made fun of me in front of the entire class.

2. Examining Costs and Benefits

Many patterns carry both advantages and drawbacks. Where attuning acknowledged the reasons to keep the problem, here both sides are held together, so the trade-off becomes the focus. The client can usually name each side already. What may be new is weighing them against one another. You can also ask directly: “What does it cost you, and what does it give you?” Examples:

  • My suspiciousness protects me, and it also pushes people away.
  • Pleasing everyone keeps me popular, and it leaves me feeling resentful.
  • Staying busy numbs the pain, and it also keeps me from dealing with my grief.

3. Uncovering Patterns and Timing

Naming repeated themes or predictable triggers helps the client see that their reactions follow a logic rather than striking at random. That recognition alone can ease the feeling of being at their mercy, and it shows where to look next. Examples:

  • Whenever someone gets close, I find a reason to pull back.
  • This anxiety shows up every time I get a new assignment at work.
  • It seems this sadness always returns around his birthday.

4. Challenging Limiting Beliefs

Once the client has recognized a belief and what it costs, phrases that question it can begin to loosen its hold. Unlike connecting past and present, the focus here is on beliefs that no longer serve the client. Examples:

  • “I’m not smart enough” was my father’s verdict when I was nine. Do I still want him grading me?
  • What would I have to see to stop believing I’ll be abandoned?
  • I’m not reliable, yet I’m the one my friends turn to when something goes wrong.

5. Exploration via Metaphor

Metaphors, similes, and analogies can capture an issue more vividly than abstract descriptions. Listen for them and use them. A metaphor the client presents usually carries more weight than one you supply because it already fits how they experience the problem. To invite the client’s own image, you can ask, “This problem is like what?” You can also judiciously offer your own. Examples:

  • It’s like my heart has a wall around it.
  • I’ve been standing guard at a door that no longer needs protecting.
  • I’m spinning my wheels in sand and can’t get enough traction to move on.

6. Investigating Secondary Gains

Unlike examining costs and benefits, where the client can already see both sides, here the benefit is one they have never named, or at least never owned. Asking what the problem prevents — what would be expected, felt, or required if it lifted — may bring a hidden payoff into view, where it can be met with clarity and compassion. Examples:

  • If I weren’t so depressed, my family would expect a lot more from me.
  • If I stay anxious about money, I won’t get complacent and end up broke again.
  • As long as I stay angry, I don’t have to feel how much I was hurt.

7. Inviting Curiosity

Anxiety pushes a client away from uncertain material. Curiosity pulls them toward it. Your own stance matters in the same way. Where pressure to “figure it out” evokes defensiveness, curiosity sustains the inquiry and carries it deeper. Examples:

  • I wonder what my body is trying to tell me with this back pain.
  • I’m curious why this keeps happening in my relationships.
  • Why can a simple email from him hijack my whole afternoon?

Seven Variations of Leading Language

Lead

As tapping reduces intensity, new space opens. Leading language points the way forward — reinforcing strengths, envisioning alternatives, and supporting change-oriented action. Because leading moves toward unknown possibilities, it works best as a collaboration — with the client actively helping to word the statements, rather than simply receiving them.

1. Reinforcing Personal Strengths

Highlight the client’s resources and resilience. Possibilities that rest on strengths the client already has feel believable rather than wishful, and the work can reach beyond recovery to fresh growth. Examples:

  • The stubbornness that got me through can now take me where I want to go.
  • My track record is much more promising than my view of myself has been.
  • What I know now that I didn’t know then is . . . (client adds the words while continuing to tap on a single point so as not to be distracted)

2. Transcending Old Beliefs

Where exploration of a longstanding belief asks “Is this still true?”, leading begins to find and articulate more adaptive perspectives, a reframe of the old belief that supports forward movement. Two approaches work especially well. One is to invite a counter-example: “Have you ever spoken up and been praised for it?” The other is to alternate: the client taps on the old belief, then on a question or freeing statement that challenges it, and then back again, so the old belief is repeatedly met by another possibility. Examples:

  • No man can be trusted . . . I’ve known men who kept their word. (alternating the two statements from one tapping point to the next)
  • Speaking up once brought rejection. Lately it’s brought us closer.
  • Doing everything alone kept me safe. Now I have people who show up.

3. Re-experiencing Past Events Safely

Once safety is established, imagination can revisit a memory while anchored in present-moment resources, helping the nervous system re-encode it from a more balanced state. Examples:

  • Even though they hurt me when I was a child, that was then, and this is now.
  • I’m bringing the safety I feel now back to my younger self in that memory.
  • I’m letting that younger part of me know she won’t always be alone.

4. Envisioning Change

Language can invite the client to imagine new ways of being. Kept achievable rather than forced, wording can make change seem plausible before it has happened. Examples:

  • I’m beginning to see myself as someone who can walk into a room full of strangers and hold my own.
  • A version of me who isn’t braced for the next problem is starting to feel possible.
  • I’m ready to . . . (client adds the words, again tapping on a single point)

5. Using Bridging Statements

Rather than leaping to some version of “I’m finally over this,” use intermediate phrases that feel believable and supportive, a reframe the client can actually accept. Examples:

  • I can’t say the anxiety is gone, but it no longer feels bigger than me.
  • Despite my mistakes, I’m starting to recognize how much I bring to my kids.
  • Trusting my own judgment is still a ways off, but I’m seeing glimpses.

6. Consolidating Gains

As a session or work on a specific issue comes to a close, naming the shifts that occurred helps them take hold. Optimistic phrasing at this point can land without resistance in a way it couldn’t during attuning and exploring. Often the client supplies the reframe on their own (“Maybe I wasn’t weak. I was a kid.”), and your phrase can simply hand it back. Examples:

  • I can see from yesterday’s staff meeting that my ability to stay strong and centered is steadily growing.
  • Something shifted today — the memory is still there, but it’s lost its grip on me.
  • I’m leaving here with a kinder way of talking to myself. And I intend to build on it.

7. Strengthening Commitment to Action

As clarity grows and emotional charge diminishes, language helps translate intention into action — not just willingness, but a concrete sense of how, and with what resources. Envisioning such specific changes helps extend the work beyond the room. Examples:

  • I’m ready to have this difficult conversation, bringing the calm I’ve found here with me.
  • I’ll say no to that last-minute request without overexplaining myself.
  • To support my recovery, I’m committing to . . . (client adds the words, again tapping on a single point)

A caution on leading

Most Powerful, Most Easily Misused. Leading is the most powerful as well as the most easily misused direction. Introduced too early — before attunement and exploration have laid the groundwork — leading statements seem presumptuous and invite resistance, or they may quietly “lead the witness” toward your idea of where healing lies, rather than following the client’s own unfolding. The remedy is collaboration: invite the client to co-construct the statements, and when unsure how a phrase landed, return to a simple “Is that right?”

Collaboration as the Remedy. As the session moves toward uncharted possibilities, invite the client to participate more actively in forming the statements used during the tapping. Here are three structured approaches for leading collaboratively:

  1. The practitioner may ask the client to finish statements such as: “A next step for me now is . . .” or “I can overcome this obstacle by . . .” or “What I understand now that I didn’t understand earlier is . . .” or “These experiences [practitioner names moments of success] tell me that I am . . .”
  2. A Gestalt-like dialogue can be initiated in which the client articulates both sides of an internal conflict while tapping (for example, “Part of me wants to leave . . .” / “And part of me is terrified to . . .”). Although this may be part of exploration and may generate insights and take some of the charge out of an inner conflict, it often leads to new levels of resolution and direction.
  3. The client can, while continuing to tap, rehearse brief statements that take conversations further or lead to resolution within challenging relationships (e.g., “Tell her what you would need if you are ever going to trust her again” or “What would you say to your father about that if he were still alive?”).

Two principles that run through several variations

Two ideas taught in most tapping trainings have no variation of their own. Each is carried by several of the twenty-one.

Reframing. Reframing is among the most powerful interventions in any psychotherapy. A reframe can begin as early as attuning, when Softening Harsh Absolutes restates a rigid statement in a more workable form. It does its fullest work in leading, through three variations in particular. Transcending Old Beliefs offers a new frame for an old rule. Using Bridging Statements offers a reframe the client can actually believe. Consolidating Gains names the new frame the client is already living. Often the most powerful reframe is the client’s own, arriving unprompted after a shift. When it does, your job is to notice it and hand it back.

Psychological Reversals. Many EFT trainings use this term for clients who want to resolve a problem while some part in them works against resolving it. You can hear it in phrases like “Nothing ever works for me,” “I don’t deserve to get over this,” or “If I let my guard down, something terrible will happen.” The self-acceptance half of the Setup Statement was designed partly with this in mind. Among the twenty-one language variations, three are especially suited for addressing psychological reversals. Validating Reasons to Keep the Problem honors what the part holding on is trying to protect. Investigating Secondary Gains can bring a hidden payoff into view. Challenging Limiting Beliefs questions the belief that keeps the client stuck.

The Framework in Practice

Attune, Explore, and Lead in a session

The following case illustrates the attune, explore, and lead directions cycling in real time. “Sarah,” a 44-year-old marketing professional, sought help for chronic anxiety that seemed to be worsening. Her nightmares and sleep disturbance had intensified over the previous six months, about the time her youngest daughter left for college. All identifying details have been altered.

Session One. Sarah described having been sexually abused by a maternal uncle between the ages of 7 and 12. Session Four. In the fourth session, which reviewed Sarah’s sleep difficulties, she reported that disturbing dreams had been few over the previous two weeks and far less intense. When she did wake from one, she could calm herself and return to sleep using a gentler form of acupoint stimulation she had learned in the previous session: holding each point while taking a deep breath, which is less activating than tapping. With the nightmares and hypervigilance receding, more of what the abuse had cost her came into view. Her vigilance had gone into protecting her children, and her days had been organized around them. That work was done. The sessions that followed turned to the life she wanted to build.

Seven Sources

Where do your words come from?

Words don’t come from a vacuum. You draw on multiple streams of information, and they feed the two mechanisms described earlier — the conceptualizer setting the direction and the formulator finding the wording. Among the natural sources for your next statement:

1. The Client’s Words and Presentation

Intake gives an initial, mutable map. The client’s presentation at the start of each session sets immediate priorities. Throughout the session, the client’s own phrasings, including comments that surface while tapping, in response to your inquiries, or at a session’s close, are the surest starting point for the next tapping statement.

2. Nonverbal Cues

Shifts in posture, facial expression, muscle tension, breathing, and eye contact give immediate feedback on how the last phrase landed, what the client is experiencing, and what the next phrase may need to address.

3. The SUD Rating

Each SUD rating, and the discussion about it after each tapping round, can be highly informative about optimal next steps. A rising SUD often means a new layer has surfaced, or what EFT calls a new aspect (a sound, an image, a feeling, a belief, or a body sensation tied to the same issue). It is a cue to return to attuning.

4. Gaps in the Story

Clients often present one layer of an issue while a deeper layer remains unspoken. If tapping isn’t moving the SUD — even when the words sound productive — something in the story may still be missing. For instance, a client focusing on situations that trigger anger may, in fact, be feeling inadequate in those situations, or the anger may trace to an earlier, similar experience, such as an old humiliation. If you have an informed guess, your next phrase can name that layer, offered as a possibility rather than a conclusion, for the client to confirm or correct.

5. Your Own Background and Clinical Experience

Life provides firsthand experience of grief, fear, and finding your way through them, which no training can offer. Your clinical experience supplies precedent for how moments like the one you are navigating tend to unfold, and for the phrasings that might serve. Much of a seasoned practitioner’s language was earned in the treatment room rather than the classroom. Experience accumulates slowly, one client at a time. Examples studied with care, like the ones in this Guide, can widen that store of precedent far faster. On the other hand, experience can reinforce an inaccurate belief, which is why the two sources below matter so much.

6. Established Knowledge

Familiarity with the mental health field’s best established practices for treating anxiety, trauma, depression, addiction, PTSD, and other challenging conditions keeps your thinking aligned with the accumulated wisdom of the clinical community. The book Tapping, which I wrote with Donna Eden, guides you through these best practices with tapping practitioners in mind.

7. Therapeutic Resonance

A pre-verbal attunement with the client can set the direction before you have reasoned it out, so the wording arrives before you have consciously formed it (more in the Beyond Words discussion).

Three Underrated Qualities

What tapping protocols quietly embody

More is going on in a tapping session than is obvious. In most talk therapy, both parties speak in paragraphs. In tapping protocols, you offer a phrase, watch what it does, and offer another — often several in the space of a minute. Each is shaped by what the last one produced: a breath that deepened, a word the client repeated more slowly, a flicker that said “not quite” or “that’s it!” A loop this fast is governed by the client in front of you, moment by moment, and all the while the emotional texture of the moment is shifting with the tapping. The material you work from — the words, the images, the goals — is the client’s. Proceeding at that speed, from that material, gives tapping protocols three qualities that other approaches have to cultivate more deliberately:

  • Tapping protocols lend themselves to a client-centered approach.
  • The collaboration between you and the client is deep, because it happens at once at the conscious, pre-verbal, and bodily levels.
  • The process makes room for the client’s own inner wisdom and capacity to heal.

Client-Centered. The words are often the client’s own, echoed back or paraphrased. The goals are the client’s. The images and memories are ones the client brought. The strengths being reinforced are ones the client has demonstrated or reported. Even the measure of progress is theirs. The SUD is a number they report, not a judgment you make. And it steers what comes next. What the client brings is welcomed without argument, including what seems contradictory or self-defeating. Even statements that lead are built from what the client has already provided. The direction you point toward is one the client has already begun to move toward, and a hypothesis that goes beyond what the client has said is offered tentatively, for the client to confirm or correct.

Deep Collaboration. What unfolds is neither something the client produces alone nor an intervention you deliver. You and the client have different roles in constructing the tapping statements. You supply the wording and the timing, drawing on what the client has presented and where the work is heading, along with a willingness to be corrected. The client says it out loud as their own. Voicing the phrase tests it on the spot. A phrase that misses is usually evident to both of you the moment it’s said. In most protocols you are also tapping along in unison. That shared rhythm may feed a physiological synchrony between clinician and client that has been found to correspond with clinical progress. Collaboration this close asks for something like a beginner’s mind, a read of the client held loosely enough that the next moment can revise it. And because neither of you fully controls where the exchange goes, a session often turns in a direction more fruitful than either of you could have planned.

Facilitated Self-Healing. This follows naturally from the client-centered nature of the work. The approach rests on a premise: the client’s deepest guidance is fundamentally trustworthy, already oriented toward healing and growth, and the client is an active participant in the work. This is not a claim that nothing is wrong. It is a claim about where the work goes, toward resolution and fuller flourishing, often by way of the parts of the story still unnamed, unprocessed, unspoken, or unfelt. Wording that reaches those parts clears the way for what is already in motion. That is part of why word choice matters so much. The closer a phrase points to blocked material, old wounds, or unrealized potentials, the more of what needs healing or cultivating falls within reach of tapping’s regulating signals. A phrase that lands loosens what was stuck and makes room. What moves into that space — healing, resilience, growth neither of you could quite have imagined at the outset — was never yours to supply. It was already the client’s, waiting for the words, and the tapping that carries them, to let it through.

The Reflective Practitioner

“Good-enough” words, offered with presence

Staying Regulated. Even if the pairing of tapping and language is a powerful tool, the practitioner is the one who has to stay present and self-aware enough to use it well. Apt words, quickly, all session long. Some facility with language is of course important, but its impact is limited unless you can remain regulated, curious, and inventive in the face of the client’s intensity and of whatever it stirs in you.

When Getting It Right Gets in the Way. In a culture of evaluation and pressure to excel, “finding the right words” can quietly become another performance — and if you’re preoccupied with getting each phrase just right, you’ve half left the room, no longer tracking the cues that could be guiding you. The more unsure you feel, the more attention the wording takes. Easing that pressure returns your attention to the client. As you accept that you’ll sometimes get the wording “wrong,” you become freer to listen, to be surprised, and to adjust. Once the direction is set, the formulator does much of the assembly on its own, sparing you the very job you were anxious about, building each phrase word by word. Your part is the direction and your presence, and the wording largely follows. Adjustments can come with the next tapping statement. The method is as forgiving as it is precise.

Showing Your Uncertainty. Wording doesn’t need to be perfect — and neither do you. There will be moments when you don’t know where the session is heading, whether the work is helping, or what the client most needs. You don’t have to hide that behind a show of confidence. Research has linked therapist genuineness to better outcomes, and genuineness, put simply, means being outwardly what you are inwardly. When voiced briefly, your uncertainty becomes something you share rather than something you manage alone. And when you aren’t presenting yourself as the one with all the answers, the client is freer to look inward for what comes next. Of course, not every doubt needs voicing. When a client needs steadiness more than candor, steadiness comes first.

Countertransference as Information. A somewhat different matter is an uncomfortable feeling a client stirs in you: irritation, protectiveness, sadness, a wish to rescue. Reflective practitioners treat these reactions, often discussed as countertransference, not as errors to be met with uneasiness or shame but as vital information about the client and about themselves. The aim is to recognize them and work with them rather than act them out. The wish to rescue is a common one. It can show up as an urge to close a door into pain before the client has walked through it. Noticed rather than obeyed, that urge becomes information, and the door stays open long enough for the tapping to do its work. When one does slip through, a misstep approached as an opening for repair rather than a failure tends to strengthen the alliance. Ruptures resolved well are associated with better outcomes. Reactions that would pull too much attention toward you belong in your own self-tapping, personal therapy, or supervision, which can also disentangle your history from client hot spots and sharpen your attunement.

Your Own Safety. Clinicians need to feel emotionally, professionally, and physically safe to offer the quality of presence this work requires. And that includes maintaining clear boundaries. If a client asks about your training, background, or personal life, you can use these inquiries to clarify roles, expectations, and the frame of the work. Instead of reacting from anxiety — over-disclosing to win trust or withdrawing defensively — feel your way to the degree of transparency that will best support the client’s therapeutic goals.

Common Tapping Errors

What to watch for

Even skilled, self-aware practitioners fall into a handful of predictable errors, and they are easier to avoid once you know how they tend to show up.

None of these feels like an error at the time. Each arrives with its own justification. Urgency feels like momentum. Easing a client’s pain too soon feels like kindness. Pressing for a result feels like diligence. Polishing the wording feels like care. Each can be readily corrected once recognized.

  1. Too little attunement
  2. Beginning to tap before the client’s concerns about the method have been addressed.
  3. Rushing the client’s words into the next tapping round without letting them land.
  4. Skipping past what the client has said to insert your own insight.
  5. Persisting with wording that sounds productive but is not working, rather than inquiring and adjusting.
  6. Becoming so absorbed in the next statement that you miss the client’s face, posture, or affect.
  7. Leading too soon
  8. Reaching for affirming or future-focused wording before the client feels met, the material has been explored, and there are signs of readiness.
  9. Closing the door on pain before the client has walked through it. When the session, or the client’s life, opens a door into emotional pain, the impulse to ease it shut with empathy and reassurance is natural. But lasting relief usually lies on the other side, and the tapping protocols are what carry the client there.
  10. Pushing for a SUD of 0 or near 0 before the work that would lower it has been done.
  11. Overlooking your own part
  12. Attributing lack of progress solely to the client — “treatment resistant,” “transference,” an “attachment disorder” — without examining your own presence, pacing, or wording.

Beyond Words

How attuned presence guides the next phrase

The deepest source of effective language may be one that precedes language entirely. The framework names the directions and the kinds of wording that serve them. But a quieter, pre-verbal process can supply the sense of what a phrase needs to accomplish, and that sense arrives before deliberation rather than from it. It informs the conceptualizer described earlier, often setting the direction before you have consciously chosen it. It is what is at work when a phrase arrives that you could not have reasoned your way to.

Many decades ago, the renowned psychologist Rollo May described how therapist and client can feel as if they share a single psychological space rather than inhabiting two separate minds. That shared space is a psychosocial background that is continuously available, and your part is to draw on it, not create it. One of May’s students, Virginia Larson, explored this phenomenon in depth, referring to it as “psychotherapeutic resonance.” She documented a merging of personal boundaries that goes well beyond ordinary empathy and rapport. In one account, Larson met a new client and, with no apparent cause, became aware of diffuse sensations deep in her own abdomen. Having ruled out any personal source, she gently asked whether the sensations might hold meaning for the client. The client immediately disclosed that she had cervical cancer and was undergoing chemotherapy — a striking illustration of how a therapist’s body can register clinically relevant information before a word is spoken. Clinicians Molly Sterling and James Bugental described this as the “meld experience”: two minds joining so that awareness is briefly, inseparably shared.

This type of deep resonance has long intrigued me. Early in my career, watching eminent therapists conduct live demonstrations for trainees, I was repeatedly struck by their capacity to offer a fresh, often surprising intervention at exactly the moment it seemed needed — frequently departing from the very methods they were known for. I watched Carl Rogers, the founder of “non-directive therapy,” become unmistakably directive, telling a client plainly that he should not marry the woman he was describing; Virginia Satir, a pioneer of family therapy, go straight to the heart of a psychodynamic issue without a word about the client’s current family or family of origin; and Alexander Lowen, the originator of bioenergetics, reach the core of an emotional difficulty without once referring to posture or bodily tension. If what they offered was not a reflection of their trademark methods, I was left asking what, exactly, they were tuning into.

One answer to what they were tuning into comes from the psychiatrist Daniel Siegel. In his framework of interpersonal neurobiology, what we usually think of as our inner life — our feelings, our level of alarm or calm, the sense we are making of a moment — is not sealed inside each of us. It is shaped continually by the people we are with, through a flow of energy and information that runs between people as well as within them. Two people in close contact keep adjusting to each other’s states, mostly without noticing. In a session, that flow runs both ways. Your nervous system registers the client’s state, which is why your own body can carry information about them. And the client’s nervous system registers yours, which is why your steadiness can help steady them.

This two-way flow is no longer only a matter of theory. Research on “interpersonal synchrony” shows that clinician and client can become physiologically attuned — in heart rate, electrodermal activity, vocalization, subtle movement, and even brain activity — and that the degree of synchrony predicts the strength of the therapeutic relationship and clinical progress. The meld described earlier, in other words, reflects shared biological processes, not only subjective rapport. That may also be part of why tapping in unison with the client matters.

Sending You Off

The compass and the map work together with the felt resonance just described. That resonance often signals where the next phrase should head before you have consciously chosen it. The compass names that direction, and the map, once you have taken it in, supplies the wording. So when you’re unsure what to say next, tend first to your own presence. Take a breath and shift your attention to the space between you and the client, which is active and informative even when nothing obvious is happening. Rather than searching for the phrase, let your attention rest there and notice the words that emerge. They will draw on whatever these pages have left in you.

Attune regularly. Explore when there is safety. Lead when there is readiness. Do that much, and good-enough words tend to come.

About this framework

The Attune / Explore / Lead framework grew out of a 10-year project spanning four lines of inquiry — a survey of experienced practitioners, a statement-by-statement coding of recorded sessions, a conceptual comparison with process research on established psychotherapies, and a computational analysis of guided tapping videos. The coding yielded 62 distinct uses of language, consolidated here into 21 variations under three higher-order directions. The survey supplied its starting categories, and the comparison and the computational analysis tested the result from outside. Both of those outside tests bore on the three directions rather than the 21 variations, which were not independently examined. It is an early but grounded account, offered as a working framework rather than a finished one. Firmer footing will require independent coders with formal inter-rater reliability, to see whether others arrive at the same 21 variations. It will also require larger and more varied samples across practitioners, clients, and presenting problems, and controlled studies linking specific language variations to treatment outcomes. At its current level of development, however, it can still serve as a flexible map and compass for orienting the next phrase.

While the map can orient you, it cannot replace the judgment, presence, and attunement that make the difference in any given moment.

This Field Guide is a companion for tapping practitioners, drawn from the freely available “Finding the Right Words” academic paper, where full methods, validation, and citations can be found. It may be freely shared. For trainings and related resources, visit energytapping.com.
