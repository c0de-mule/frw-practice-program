Subject: What to say at the next tapping point — new research and a free Field Guide

[Open with a note from you if you wish. A sentence in your own voice about why you’re passing this on will do more than anything written here.]

Every tapping practitioner meets the same moment again and again: the client has just spoken, the next tapping point is waiting, and you have a couple of seconds to decide what to say. Those words are among the few things in the room actually within your control, and they matter. Whatever you say next can deepen the work, stall it, or inadvertently close a door the client has just opened.

Picture a client whose youngest has just left for college. “I’m being silly. It’s a good thing,” she says, blinking hard. Repeat her words back, and you confirm the voice telling her not to feel it. Offer “This ache of the suddenly quiet house,” and she finally can.

David Feinstein, PhD, has spent the past decade studying that moment, and his findings about the most effective language during tapping sessions are just out in Frontiers in Psychology. He has distilled them into a practical Field Guide for anyone who guides others in tapping, professionally or informally. Every choice of words rests on a clinical choice about where the client is and what the moment calls for, so the Guide is also about reading a client and pacing a tapping session. Its 40 pages are laid out for easy reading, with short sections and plenty of examples, so it reads in about an hour. It’s free and complete on its own.

The Guide spells out findings most practitioners have never seen articulated. Three of them:

  • Effective tapping statements move the session in one of three directions: they attune (meet the client where they are), explore (open up what lies beneath), or lead (move toward change). The research identified seven kinds of wording that carry a session forward in each direction.
  • You don’t compose your words one at a time. Once you know where you want to go, the words arrive on their own, at the speed of conversation, from all the language you’ve taken in over the years. What you take in shapes what comes out, the way people who read good writing tend to write better. That is why simply reading the Guide’s many examples of effective wording starts to improve what comes to you in the moment.
  • Good wording comes from noticing the right things: the client’s own words, their body language, how distressed they rate themselves (the SUD rating), what’s missing from their story, your own experience, and your intuitive attunement to the client. The Guide shows how each one points to the next phrase.
