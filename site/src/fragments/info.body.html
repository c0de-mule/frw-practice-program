More Than Knowing
the Protocol

A client’s distress can be plain while the most useful response is not. Should you acknowledge the emotion? Invite further exploration? Help the client consider a different perspective? Even a well-intentioned phrase can be premature, too general, or poorly matched to what the client is actually feeling.

Experienced therapists develop a feel for these distinctions over years of practice. The Practice Program lets you examine and rehearse them systematically, with realistic clinical situations and the reasoning behind every option.

The practice is built to help you

  • Keep the client in contact with what they actually feel, rather than talking about it from a distance.
  • Know when to stay with a feeling and when the client is ready to move toward change.
  • Catch your own habitual missteps, such as leading too soon, that usually only supervision reveals.
  • Stay present with the client instead of searching for words.

How it works

How Practice Trains the Brain

1

Make the call

Read the moment and commit to a phrase, before anything is revealed. You can formulate the phrase first and then see the choices, or start by viewing the choices.

2

See what each option would do

Including the ones you didn’t choose, and what each option would have cost or gained.

3

Learn the pattern, not the answer

Every correct choice is identified as one of the Field Guide’s 21 variations, and the reasoning shows exactly what makes it that one.

Each item drops you into a true-to-life clinical moment, with options that run from clinical errors to solid choices to fine distinctions that ordinarily require substantial clinical experience. The wrong answers are explained as carefully as the right ones, because that is where much of the learning happens. By the end of the program, the choosing will have become quicker and surer, with the right words arriving on their own more and more often.

No single perfect phrase exists. Many phrasings could serve the client in that moment, though some will serve better than others. The learning is in coming to recognize what makes the best one work.

Language researchers distinguish choosing what to say, the conceptualizer, from putting it into words, the formulator, which works at two or three words a second, far too fast to choose words one at a time. The scenarios train the choice of direction, and the reasoning behind all 528 options adds to the patterns the formulator draws on. You don’t need to remember the details afterward. What stays is a change in which words come to mind when it’s your turn to speak. In short, the program trains your brain to find the most effective phrase.

More than language. Every choice of words rests on a clinical decision about where the client is and what the moment calls for. So the program is also practice in reading a client and pacing a tapping session.

The program

Three Tiers of Clinical Skill

Each tier builds on the last, from recognizing what the moment calls for, to finding the words that fit, to the fine distinctions that help clients move through what has kept them stuck.

Tier One · Foundations

Sensing the Direction

At the foundation: recognizing what the moment is asking for. You will learn to read the moment across a wide range of clinical situations, until the needed direction (attune, explore, or lead) comes more readily.

Tier Two · Craft

Shaping the Phrase

More challenging: the direction (attune, explore, lead) is clear, but many wordings are possible. You practice finding the phrase that actually carries the direction forward most effectively among options that all look reasonable.

Tier Three · Mastery

Sophisticated Discernment

The finest grain: choices so close that the difference turns on particulars in the scene — the client’s exact words, where the arousal sits, what the session has already done. You practice the discriminations that separate a wording that may sound right from the one the moment actually calls for.

What’s inside

Everything, in One Guided Path

The wrong answers teach, too

Every incorrect option carries its own explanation — a well-intentioned move offered too early, a beginner’s overreach, a phrase that sounds clinically astute but doesn’t advance the session. This is deliberate: fine discrimination is built one near-miss at a time. Threaded through the explanations are brief clinical asides — observations that catch a familiar moment from an unexpected angle.

A few hours to complete, a reference you keep

The first pass through each tier takes about an hour. Retrying what you missed and reflecting on it add a little more. You move through them at your own pace. Lifetime access means the scenarios stay yours to work again — these discriminations stay sharp by being exercised.

Grounded in research

Built directly on the Attune / Explore / Lead framework — and its 21 variations of language — from peer-reviewed research. Each correct answer names the variation it reflects, so the framework becomes yours scenario by scenario. Each item also closes with a takeaway that states the principle at work as a plain rule of thumb, one that will serve you in a session whether or not you remember which variation it belongs to.

Your Practice Profile

At the end of each tier you see where your choices landed: across the three directions, across the 21 variations, and against the errors practitioners most often make. Most people find a tendency — leading a little too soon, or staying in attunement past the point where the client was ready to move. You finish knowing your own tendencies, which would usually be a benefit of live supervision.

Your own words first

Before the options appear, you can write what you would say. After you answer, you compare your phrase with the best one. Recognizing the right words is one skill. Producing them with a client waiting is another. This lets you practice both. A good way to use it is to go through the program once with the options showing, then return a few weeks later and go through again writing your own phrase first. The language patterns your formulator was exposed to the first time through will guide you, as they will already be doing in your sessions with clients.

Into your own practice

After each tier, three short questions ask you to name a pattern to watch for based on your responses to the scenarios. Then picture or imagine a client in whom the same issue is active and write the phrase you’d use. A second pass lets you retry the items you missed. Your Practice Record gathers your scores, reflections, and phrases to print or save.

How the program came about

Built on a Career Studying Therapeutic Language

David Feinstein, PhD, has been investigating the therapeutic uses of language since his early work on the construction of personal reality while serving on the faculty of the Department of Psychiatry at The Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine in the 1970s, work that led to an award-winning book. Since learning of tapping protocols and recognizing their power two decades ago, he has been studying the most effective wording to accompany them. A comprehensive report, which provides the scientific rationale for this program, was recently published in the peer-reviewed journal Frontiers in Psychology.

Dr. Feinstein is a clinical psychologist whose work developing innovative therapeutic approaches has earned nine national awards for his books on consciousness and healing, including the 2017 USA Book News Award for the Best Psychology Book and the 2026 Nautilus Book Award Gold in Psychology. In May 2026 he received the Association for Comprehensive Energy Psychology’s Distinguished Pioneer Award. With his wife Donna Eden, he has built one of the world’s largest organizations teaching the hands-on use of Energy Medicine. Together they have authored several award-winning books, including The Promise of Energy Psychology, Tapping, and the New York Times bestseller The Energies of Love.

Integrating tapping increased the speed, depth, and range of positive outcomes in his practice. Conveying to others how he did this was another matter. He reflects, “I never tried to teach my approach to tapping because it builds on thirty years of clinical experience prior to my learning the technique. How do you teach that in a tapping course?” But when he ran process research on his own videotaped tapping sessions, he found that the language patterns moving those sessions forward were finite and could be organized in a way that can be taught. His question had an answer after all: the thirty years could not be taught, but the language they had built into his tapping sessions could.

The map behind it

What the Field Guide Maps

Brief enough to hold

Short enough to be spoken, repeated, or held in mind while tapping — and when a thought needs more room, spread it across several tapping points.

Concrete and close

Specific sensory language, often based on the client’s own words, that keeps the client in contact with the actual feeling rather than narrating it from a distance.

Offered, not imposed

The practitioner supplies the phrase, but it only works if the client finds that it fits. When a phrase goes beyond what the client has said, their response is the test — in the body, in how they take it up, or in what they say when you ask if it fits.

Good enough is enough

Wording need not be polished or perfect. Offered with presence and a willingness to adjust, good-enough phrasing can carry the work forward. Whatever one phrase misses, the next can pick up.

Before You Start

How long does it take?

About six hours in all. The first time through each tier takes about an hour, three hours for all three. Retrying items you missed might add another 30 minutes. The reflections after each tier take about an hour total. Reading the Field Guide and the Guide on a Page takes about 90 minutes. No need to do it in one sitting. Progress is saved automatically.

Does access expire?

No. Access is permanent, so you can come back for a refresher before a difficult case, or whenever you want to freshen your language skills. Any updates to the program are incorporated each time you open it.

What do I need?

Any browser on a computer, tablet, or phone. Nothing to install. You only need to be familiar with at least one tapping protocol.

Do I need the Field Guide first?

Not required. Anyone experienced with tapping can work straight through the program. But the Field Guide is where the framework is taught: the three directions, the 21 variations, what makes each one work, and the importance of presence. The program is where it becomes yours. Reading the Field Guide first means you get to spend the program building the skill rather than learning the map.

Can I try before buying?

Yes. Ten scenarios — the one offered above plus nine more — are free to work through, with the full reasoning behind every option. About ten minutes, and it will tell you more than any description can. Start with the sample →

Is it suitable for non-clinicians?

The scenarios are written from clinical practice, but the skills apply to anyone who guides others in tapping — in a coaching relationship, in a group, or with friends and family.

Will it conflict with the protocol I was trained in?

No. Whatever protocol you use, you already decide what to say each time it’s your turn to speak. The program gives that choice an expanded vocabulary and a place to practice it.

Does it replace clinical training or supervision?

No. It provides structured practice in reading clinical cues, choosing a direction, and finding language that fits the client’s experience. It complements training programs in acupoint tapping protocols such as EFT.

Does completing it provide certification?

No. The program offers continuing education hours through ACEP, but it does not confer a certification or credential.

Are the scenarios based on actual clients?

They are clinically representative composites, written to reflect situations practitioners meet in tapping sessions. They are not records of individual clients.

How do I know it works?

The framework rests on a decade of research recently published in Frontiers in Psychology, and the program is built directly on it. What has not been done is a study linking the program itself to practitioner performance. Early users report finding it highly valuable, which is encouraging but is not the same thing as research evidence. So the honest answer is that the framework is well grounded but the program’s effect on the clients of practitioners who have completed it has yet to be measured. Stronger validation will require independent coders, larger samples, and controlled studies linking specific language variations to outcomes. The most direct answer available to you is to work the ten free scenarios and judge for yourself. And if the Practice Program isn’t right for you, you can ask for a full refund within 14 days of purchase.

Isn’t it best to stay with the client’s own words?

Often, yes — and that is the safest ground with a new client or when arousal is high. But echoing is also a craft. Which word to give back, which to soften, when to stop talking altogether — these are choices, and making them well is vital for attunement. As the practitioner goes beyond what the client has presented, the Field Guide teaches that speculative statements are hypotheses, to be checked in the moment, such as with a simple “True or false?” or “Is that right?” The Field Guide also gives considerable space to presence and therapeutic resonance. Both support attunement and guard against unconsciously imposing the practitioner’s own story on the client’s unprocessed material.

Learning objectives

After completing this program, participants will be able to:

  1. Describe the role of therapist language in acupoint tapping, including how wording can best direct the focus of the tapping.
  2. Identify the three directions an effective tapping phrase can take, and classify examples of the language variations within each.
  3. Identify and justify the direction a clinical moment calls for, based on the strength of rapport, the client’s SUD rating, and readiness for change.
  4. Select wording that serves a given direction while recognizing common errors of wording and timing, such as leading before the client’s issues have been adequately explored.
  5. Evaluate several plausible phrasings for the same clinical moment and explain why one fits best.
  6. Apply the program’s principles to new clinical vignettes, including revising a tapping statement that misses the moment.
